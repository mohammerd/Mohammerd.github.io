---
title: "When Knowledge Becomes Accessible, What Is the Purpose of College?"
date: "2026-11-24"
description: "The internet and AI are changing how we learn. Universities need to rethink what they teach, how they assess students, and what makes a degree valuable."
---

For decades, college has been treated as one of the main routes to a successful life. We are told to attend school, earn a degree, and find a good job. People who take a different path can quickly be labelled uneducated or even illiterate, as though a degree is the only proof that someone has learned anything.

But when knowledge is easier to access than ever, what exactly is the purpose of college?

To be honest, my own experience has been disappointing. Most of what I have learned about technology, artificial intelligence, and the subjects that interest me came from the internet. I spent more time learning independently than I did in classrooms. I barely attended classes, and the classroom was not where most of my learning happened.

I was more interested in building things than simply attending lectures. I wanted to start a company. I spent time teaching myself, experimenting with AI tools, using what people now call “vibe coding” to turn ideas into prototypes, and asking AI to explain concepts I did not understand. I could move from an idea to an experiment without waiting for a course or lecturer to put it on the timetable.

That experience made building feel more possible, but it also taught me that making a prototype is not the same as building a company. You still need to understand a real problem, speak to users, work with other people, make difficult decisions, and learn from failure. Technology can lower the barrier to starting; it does not remove the work required to build something useful.

For me, college was more valuable as a social environment: meeting people, making friends, learning together, having conversations, and developing confidence and public-speaking skills. Those things matter. But should the academic part of college be doing more?

## The internet changed access. AI is changing learning.

A student with an internet connection can find university lectures, research papers, courses, books, technical documentation, and communities working on the same problems. AI tools add another layer. They can explain a difficult idea in different ways, give examples, help debug code, quiz a student, and provide feedback at any time.

That does not make independent learning effortless, or AI reliable by default. AI systems can be wrong and give learners a false sense of understanding. Students still need to check sources, practise, solve problems themselves, and explain what they have learned. Access is also unequal: devices, reliable internet, electricity, language support, and time remain out of reach for many people.

Universities still have something important to offer: structure, expert guidance, research facilities, recognised qualifications, and communities that are difficult to build alone.

## Universities need to adapt

I do not think banning AI is a serious long-term education strategy. Universities should teach students how to use it responsibly in their own disciplines. Law students should learn to verify AI-assisted research. Engineering students should learn where AI tools help and where calculations need independent checking. Humanities students should learn to use AI for exploration without outsourcing their judgement.

Assessment needs to change too. If an assignment can be completed by copying a prompt into a chatbot, perhaps the assignment is measuring the wrong thing. Practical projects, presentations, oral examinations, research, and discussions can reveal whether students understand a subject. Lecturers should become guides and critical mentors, not simply people who deliver information.

## What might college look like in ten years?

This is a prediction, not a certainty. Over the next decade, I expect AI tutors to become a normal part of learning. Courses may become more personalised, and students may combine university classes with online learning, independent projects, internships, and shorter professional credentials.

Universities that connect knowledge with practical work, mentorship, research, and strong communities will still have something valuable to offer. Institutions that mainly deliver lectures, set memorisation-based exams, and issue certificates without adapting may face harder questions about their value. Degrees will still matter in many professions, and universities will remain important for research and recognised qualifications. College is unlikely to disappear, but the old model will face more competition.

I still believe education matters. But education and attending college are not the same thing. The internet made knowledge easier to reach; AI is making it more interactive. Universities now need to show why learning with them is worth the time, effort, and money.

If knowledge is becoming accessible to almost everyone, what will make a university worth attending?
